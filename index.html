Final-year PhD candidate in Machine Learning

Jennifer Hsia

Carnegie Mellon University

I study language model behavior and evaluation, with a focus on grounded systems. My research asks why models fail even when the information they need is available, develops targeted evaluations to identify those failures, and explores interventions that help models use evidence more reliably.

I am advised by Prof. Aarti Singh and Prof. Zachary Lipton. I received a B.S.E. in Computer Science from Princeton University, where my undergraduate research was advised by Prof. Peter Ramadge.

I am currently seeking research scientist roles focused on model behavior, evaluation, and reliable grounded systems.

Current

Research

Reliable evidence use in grounded assistants

My current work studies a failure mode in which assistants give advice supported by retrieved evidence without first checking whether that evidence applies to the user's situation. I am developing evaluations and interventions that test whether models establish applicability before responding.

Selected

Publications

Deciding Among Plausible Answers: Joint Constraint Aggregation for Retrieval-Augmented QA

EMNLP 2026

Jennifer Hsia, Zachary Lipton, Aarti Singh

When retrieval surfaces several plausible answers, models often favor the most frequently supported candidate even when it violates a qualifier in the question. JCA decomposes questions into explicit constraints and jointly aggregates evidence across candidates.

RAGGED: Towards Informed Design of Scalable and Stable RAG Systems

ICML 2025

Jennifer Hsia, Afreen Shaikh, Zora Zhiruo Wang, Graham Neubig

Proceedings arXiv Code BibTeX

Across retrievers, rerankers, prompts, and retrieval depths, RAG system stability is governed primarily by the reader's robustness to noisy context, not retriever quality alone.

Goodhart's Law Applies to NLP's Explanation Benchmarks

Findings of EACL 2024

Jennifer Hsia, Danish Pruthi, Aarti Singh, Zachary Lipton

Proceedings arXiv Code BibTeX

We show that common faithfulness metrics can be improved by simple wrappers without improving the underlying explanations, exposing a gap between benchmark scores and the behavior those metrics are intended to measure.
